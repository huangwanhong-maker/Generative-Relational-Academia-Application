# Notes — Paper II: *Incentives, Collective Action, and Adoption in GRA Infrastructure*

**Author:** Wanhong Huang · working draft, 2026 · CC BY-NC 4.0 · 40 pp, 17 sections
Source: [papers/GRA-paper-II-...pdf](../papers/GRA-paper-II-Incentives-Collective-Action-and-Adoption.pdf)

**Role in the series:** the *economic and institutional* paper. Paper I says the record is worth
keeping; Paper IV says how to keep it. This one asks **whether any of it will actually happen** —
and answers, honestly, that nothing here gives reason to expect it will.

> This is the paper that keeps the project honest. **Most of its content is a list of things the
> project may not claim.** Read it before writing any pitch, README, or grant application.

---

## 0. What the paper says it can and cannot secure

> A protocol **can** alter the distribution of costs and make certain acts cheap. It **cannot**
> create an incentive where none exists, **cannot** dissolve competition for a positional good, and
> **cannot** supply a norm a community has not adopted.

It also explicitly declines the standard rhetorical move: it does **not** argue that the present
arrangement is so defective that any alternative is worth trying. The incumbent performs two
functions that must be preserved — concentration of resources on evidenced capacity, and priority as
the reward making early disclosure rational.

**Requirement 2.3 (evidential marking).** Every claim is marked **formal** (follows from stated
assumptions) / **empirical** (cited studies, population + strength stated) / **conjecture**.
Strength of the literatures used, descending: positional goods + contest theory (formal) → assessment
reliability + cost of competition (empirical, reasonably strong) → **what makes research
infrastructure adopted (thin, retrospective, selected-on-outcome)**.

> **Requirement 2.4 (Standard for a solution).** A difficulty is treated as *addressed* only where
> the paper states **a mechanism**, identifies **the party who bears its cost**, and names **the
> condition under which the mechanism fails**. A mechanism without an identified cost-bearer is
> recorded as **unaddressed**.

This is stricter than the field's practice, and deliberately: describing an arrangement in which
someone does work benefiting others *without saying why they would* is the characteristic failure
that produced the non-adopted systems of Paper I §8.

**Terms.** *Adoption* = begins conducting work under the protocol at a declared tier **and
continues**; one trajectory then stopping is not adoption. *Overhead* = effort that would not have
been expended without the arrangement, measured against the participant's own purposes. *Private
benefit* = benefit accruing to the cost-bearer, at their own scale, **without the participation of
others**.

## §3 Where the commons dilemma actually is

> **Claim 3.1 (formal).** The **tragedy of the commons has no object in knowledge.** That analysis
> concerns a rival resource degraded by uncoordinated appropriation; no quantity of reading, citing,
> absorbing or forking diminishes what a trajectory record holds. Transferring the tragedy imports a
> mechanism whose precondition is absent.

Not pedantry: misdiagnosing the dilemma as appropriation invites appropriation-type remedies —
**restrictions on use** — and Paper III shows restriction is justified only on grounds *none of
which is overuse*.

> **Claim 3.2 (formal).** Knowledge is a public good, so the predicted failure is **underprovision,
> not overuse** (Olson 1965). Remedies that increase the cost of *using* knowledge address a failure
> that does not obtain and worsen the one that does.

**The four subtractable goods** (this is where Ostrom's design principles apply — *not* to the
knowledge produced from them):

| Good | Character |
|---|---|
| **Review / assessment capacity** | The time of a party competent to judge. Rival, limited, unpaid. **The one the design most directly strains.** |
| **Maintainer labour** | Keeping the record coherent, resolving duplicates, curating the open register, answering newcomers (Eghbal 2016). |
| **The instrument** | Rival in the ordinary sense, where the work needs one (→ Paper III). |
| **Community trust** | Willingness to extend good faith to unknown contributors. Consumed by bad experiences, replenished slowly. An open entry path draws on it directly. |

> **Claim 3.3.** The protocol **lowers the cost of contributing and lowers the cost of assessing by
> nothing.**

**Rationing without a ranking (Claim 3.4).** Congestion is ordinarily managed by ranking; the
no-scalar rule removes it. Three alternatives, all **charter-level, none supplied by the protocol**:
*reciprocal obligation* (no deployed instance at scale) · *bounded intake* (rations without ordering;
displaces the difficulty onto the administrator; wastes arbitrarily) · *maintainer triage* (what OSS
does; efficient; reintroduces exactly the position Paper I §15 governs). **Recorded as unaddressed.**

**The inverse risk (Claim 3.5).** Fine-grained attribution across many small contributions resembles
the biomedical-patent anticommons (Heller & Eisenberg 1998). Answered by **excluding any veto**: an
absorption link confers *attribution and no power to prevent, condition or reverse use* — the
moral-rights construction. **Complete against legal fragmentation; incomplete against social
fragmentation** (a community may treat attribution claims as though they carried veto power whatever
the spec says — *conjecture*).

## §4 Review capacity — the binding constraint

**Claim 4.1 (formal).** The protocol shifts the ratio of contributing-cost to assessing-cost toward
more demand on assessment. **Congestion is a consequence of the design *working*, not of its
failing.**
**Claim 4.2.** Assessment capacity is fixed in the short run, unpaid, provided under a norm rather
than a contract, and **already strained**.
**Claim 4.3.** The protocol **makes assessment activity visible and provides no assessment
capacity.** Visibility is a *precondition* for a community to run a reciprocity norm; it is not such
a norm.
> **Claim 4.4.** Congestion of assessment capacity is **the difficulty in this series least addressed
> by the design and most likely to determine whether a conforming arrangement survives at scale.**
> Every available remedy places an **unfunded burden on a small number of parties**.

## §5 Maintainer labour

The non-research work: registering others' transitions · resolving duplicate/near-duplicate states ·
curating the open register *so it stays a list of open problems and not a list of everything nobody
has closed* · answering entrants · maintaining charter, classes, membership · custody, deposit,
succession.

> **Claim 5.2 (empirical).** Open-source is **not** evidence that voluntary maintenance of shared
> infrastructure is sustainable. It is evidence that such maintenance **concentrates on very few,
> frequently unpaid parties** and **fails silently until something breaks** (Eghbal 2016; Shaw & Hill
> 2014). *Usually cited as encouragement; it reads as a warning.*

**Two disanalogies, running opposite ways:** software has tests, so a maintainer's per-contribution
assessment cost is low — **assessing a challenge to a claim has no such shortcut**, so maintenance
here is *more* expensive per unit. And software has a large dependent *commercial* user population
that sometimes funds maintenance — **a trajectory record has no such population**, so the OSS funding
argument does not transfer.

**Claim 5.3.** No party's interest is proportional to the benefit it would receive (institutions gain
evidence of contribution, funders gain selection information, unaffiliated scholars gain visibility —
all diffuse, against concentrated cost). Olson's **privileged-provider exception** names both how
comparable infrastructure actually gets funded *and* the failure mode: **the paying party's purposes
become the arrangement's purposes.**

Protocol's contribution (none of it a solution): makes maintenance **visible** (precondition for
compensation, not compensation) · **bounds the maintainer's authority via portability** — *at a cost
to the maintainer, who then holds the burdens of authority and few of its returns* · **permits** the
work to be distributed. **Recorded as unaddressed.**

## §6 The positional bound — *the section that deletes claims*

**Claim 6.1 (formal).** The aggregate quantity of standing in a field is **fixed independently** of
how much work is produced or how visible contributions are made. Any arrangement making more
contributions visible **redistributes standing; it does not increase it.**

> **Claim 6.2 (formal).** The series **may claim** that the arrangement changes *what parties compete
> for*. It **may not claim** that it reduces competition, dissolves hierarchy, or increases the
> recognition available to those who lack it — **any passage implying otherwise is an error to be
> corrected.**

Consequences: attribution mechanisms are **redistributive**, so a party who presently benefits from
the invisibility of others' contributions **is made worse off** — *a reason to expect resistance, not
an argument against the mechanism*. Openness alters *who may enter*, not *what is competed for*.

**Two dimensions actually open to alteration:** (1) **the basis of comparison** — artefact counts vs
recorded contribution to changes in understanding; available *because* the artefact has weakened as
evidence (Paper I §11); (2) **the visibility of contribution** — but standing rises only if the
community counts it, which returns to (1).

**Claim 6.4 — the objection that this proves too much fails.** What is fixed is the *aggregate of
standing*. What is **not** fixed: which activities produce it, who may compete, what evidence is
admissible, and **what is lost when work is not recorded**. Retention, reuse of abandoned material,
and interpretability of a decision years later **are not bounded by the positional argument at all**.
Keep the two sets of claims apart.

## §7–8 Selection, dissipation, and the absorption test

**Definition 7.1 / Claim 7.2 (formal).** An open selection is an **all-pay contest**: effort is sunk
whether or not the entrant wins. Opening it to more entrants **increases aggregate dissipation** —
and disproportionately burdens those for whom preparation is a larger share of available time. *An
arrangement where many develop proposals in the open and the rest are discarded is the incumbent
arrangement with a wider entrance and a higher aggregate cost.*
Neither standard rebuttal closes the gap (**Claim 7.3**): the *learning* response identifies a gain
that is **private, unrecorded, and lost when the entrant moves on** — precisely the condition this
series exists to address; the *signalling* response is bounded by the evidence on assessment
reliability.

> **Definition 8.1.** An arrangement is **competitive** where the work of unselected entrants
> **leaves no trace** in the work that proceeds. It is **generative** where content originating in
> unselected work **enters the work that proceeds and is attributed to the party who produced it.**
>
> The definition turns on **what becomes of unselected work** — not on openness of entry, number of
> participants, or manner of decision. *An open arrangement discarding losing proposals is
> competitive. A closed arrangement that takes up and credits a rejected proposal's method is
> generative.*

> **Requirement 8.2 (The absorption test).** Examine the record of the work that proceeded and count
> the transitions whose content originated in work that was **not selected**, with attribution to the
> originating party. **Where such transitions are absent, the arrangement is competitive whatever its
> constitution states.**

Three properties make it usable: **checkable** from the absorption links the spec already requires
(no new instrument); **falsifiable against the proposal itself** — any conforming arrangement may be
examined and found to absorb nothing; and **applicable to existing arrangements** without the protocol
(laboriously — a funding programme, a lab, a journal can be asked whether the work that proceeded took
up content from what it declined).

**Claim 8.3.** The absorption test is **the folding criterion of the companion papers applied at the
point of selection** — *the series employs one criterion at four positions, not four criteria.*
(Value returning to the relation that produced it = generative; value accruing to an attractor
outside that relation = not.)

**Four limits (Claim 8.4).** Absorption is **necessary, not sufficient** (an arrangement may absorb
diligently and produce nothing of value). It may be **performed badly** — content taken up in a way
that misrepresents what the originator held, recording a debt they would not recognise (hence the
spec's **contested attribution** provision). **Counting is forbidden** — an absorption count is a
scalar, farmable within a season by nominal absorptions; the test is applied *by a reader reaching a
judgement*, never computed or displayed. And it says nothing about **generative capacity** — two
arrangements may absorb identically while the relations differ entirely.

## §9 Staged selection and backward induction

The natural design: (1) open work, no ranking, diversity encouraged → (2) collective development with
objection and revision → (3) selection and allocation.

> **Claim 9.1 (formal).** Where a later stage allocates a scarce resource and everyone knows it, the
> earlier stages **are not unranked — they are ranked with a delay.** A declaration that a stage is
> non-competitive does not make it non-competitive. *(Robust: it suffices that some participants
> anticipate the selection; their behaviour imposes the same pressure on the rest.)*

**Two predicted behaviours (Claim 9.2):**
- **Withholding** — a participant with a strong idea discloses late. **The exploration stage
  therefore fills with the material participants are least concerned about, which is the material
  least worth having.**
- **Adversarial objection** — improving a rival's line raises the probability the rival is selected,
  so the rational act is **to object in ways that damage rather than in ways that improve** — and
  objection is exactly the mechanism the arrangement relies on for its generative benefit.

> **Claim 9.3 — the repair.** Where content absorbed into a selected line is **attributed** to its
> producer, and the attribution is **recorded durably and recognised**, contributing to a rival's
> line carries a **positive expected return**, and improving objection carries a return that damaging
> objection does not. **Attributive absorption is the condition under which a staged design survives
> backward induction — not a courtesy extended to unselected parties.**

Notes: it operates more strongly on **development** than exploration (an improving objection is
credited immediately; an early-disclosed idea is protected only by registration, which converts
exposure into a dated claim *and generates nothing*). And it **requires the attribution to be worth
something** — a community counting only selected outcomes leaves the repair inoperative.

> **Claim 9.4.** Staged selection without attributive absorption is **worse than an undisguised
> competition**, since it obtains the appearance of openness while producing the behaviour of a
> contest.

## §10 Sparsity and plurality of selection criteria

**Claim 10.1.** Any criterion applied **often enough and singly enough** becomes the description
under which participants organise their work. *The problem is frequency and singularity, not the
choice of criterion.*
> **Definition 10.2.** **Sparsity** = the interval between selection events, measured against the
> interval over which a line of work develops. **Plurality** = the number of applied criteria **that
> cannot be combined into a single ordering.**

**Claim 10.3.** Sparse → weak optimisation pressure between events. Plural → no single ordering
forms. **Frequent + single-criterion = worst case.**
**Claim 10.4 (conjecture).** **Plurality is not preserved under frequent application** — participants
infer the operative criterion from repeated outcomes. **So sparsity is a *condition* of plurality,
not an independent parameter.**

**Three costs.** Sparse selection **concentrates risk** on those with short horizons — again the
least-standing population. It is **administratively unattractive** (frequent small allocations are
easier to justify and correct, and produce a steady record of activity). Plural criteria are
**contestable** — the decision cannot be defended as following mechanically from the criteria, so the
decision-maker's judgement becomes visible. *A cost by ordinary standards; an advantage under this
series' analysis, since a visible judgement is attributable to a party.*

**Claim 10.5.** Both are **charter parameters, not protocol parameters**. The protocol makes their
values visible after the fact and supplies no means of setting them. **Unaddressed at the level of
the design.**

## §11 Overhead and its incidence

**Claim 11.1.** Overhead concentrates in **acts requiring articulation**, specifically **the decision
act**. Typing and registration are cheap by construction (a small number of narrow choices under the
factorisation; a single affirmative action). **Recording *why* a direction was abandoned, at the
moment of abandoning it, is not — and no property of an interface reduces that cost.**

> **Claim 11.2 (formal).** Overhead falls hardest on participants with **the least time and the least
> standing — the population the series' diagnosis concerns.** The arrangement is **regressive in the
> dimension the series most cares about, whatever its intentions.**

**The byproduct principle**, restated economically: an objection is raised because the objector wants
a claim changed; a release is declared because the author wants something citable; a connection is
recorded because the connector wants the relation known.
> **Strongest form:** the arrangement records **what participants already produce and discard** —
> weekly progress reports, supervision notes, meeting summaries are *compressed accounts of a
> trajectory produced routinely under present conditions*. Adding a little structure to work already
> performed imposes far less than asking for new work.

> **Claim 11.3.** The byproduct principle reduces the overhead of every act **except the decision
> act**, whose purpose for the performer is weakest. A participant abandoning a direction gains
> little from recording why; the party who gains is a stranger years later. **This is the disparity
> that defeated earlier systems, surviving in one act after the design removed it from the others.**

> **Requirement 11.4 (Private-benefit sufficiency).** An arrangement is adoptable **only where its
> minimal form is worth adopting by a single participant with no other participants present.** An
> arrangement whose benefits appear only at scale is never adopted, since no party is ever in a
> position to obtain them.

Historical analogue: **version control was adopted by programmers working alone, for private reasons**;
the collaborative benefits followed. *Nobody adopted it out of commitment to a social model of
software production.* (Claim 11.5 — conjecture by analogy, and the analogy is to a case with a **far
more favourable cost structure**.)

**Claim 11.6.** Residual **regressivity is unaddressed**: the private-benefit condition addresses the
*timing* of benefits, not their *distribution*. A senior party adopting the personal tier loses
little; a precarious party obtains the same record and loses time they cannot spare. Compensating it
would require a funding party no analysis identifies.

## §12 Selection pressure on adopting groups — the strongest argument against

> **Claim 12.1 (formal, the dominance argument).** In an environment rewarding output rate, a group
> adopting an arrangement that imposes **any net cost** is disadvantaged relative to groups that do
> not, **irrespective of the arrangement's epistemic merits**. Sustained, adopting groups are
> **selected against.**

Hence adoption is not a matter of persuasion: *a group persuaded of the value and situated in this
environment is a group persuaded to accept a competitive disadvantage, and persuasion of that kind
does not survive a funding cycle.* And that collective benefits exceed collective costs is **not in
dispute and not relevant** — no group receives the collective benefit; every adopting group bears its
own cost.

**Two escapes only:**
1. **Net private benefit** at the adopting party's own scale. Candidate components, stated as an
   economic claim rather than a feature list: *time recovered in preparing progress reports and
   presentations from an existing record* · *effort saved in not repeating an abandoned approach* ·
   *faster onboarding of a new member from the record instead of from conversation*. **Whether these
   exceed the overhead is an empirical question nobody has measured.**
2. **Insulation** from output-rate selection — theory groups with secure funding, nonprofit research
   bodies, interdisciplinary institutes, retired scholars, unaffiliated researchers.

> **Claim 12.2.** Adoption is predicted to **begin among insulated parties** and spread to others only
> where net private benefit is demonstrated. **The prediction is uncomfortable for the series, since
> insulated parties are peripheral to the fields whose practice the arrangement would have to change.**

**Claim 12.4 (the honesty condition).** The series **may not argue for adoption on the ground that
the arrangement benefits scholarship collectively.** That argument is available to no individual
party, and **its repetition is the characteristic error of proposals in this field.** What may be
argued: that a particular party, at a particular scale, obtains more than it gives up — **and that
argument requires evidence this series does not have.**

## §13 Network externalities and the tier structure

**Claim 13.1.** Benefits rising with participation → a coordination problem where **the return to the
first adopter is lowest**; where the benefit is *entirely* of this kind, the arrangement **has no
first adopter and does not begin**. *This is a second, independent explanation for the non-adoption of
the 1970– systems: a party constructing an argument map alone obtains a map, and the promised value
lay in a network that never formed.*

Three ordinary remedies, all unavailable: **subsidy** (needs a funding party whose interest is
proportional — §5 established there is none) · **mandate** (converts the arrangement into a
compliance requirement, and the mandating party's purposes become the arrangement's) · **bundling
with an already adopted good** — *the most promising, and layering over version control and
repositories is the attempt; success unknown*.

> **Claim 13.2.** Where benefits are **stratified so each tier returns something at the scale of that
> tier**, the coordination problem decomposes into a sequence of decisions **a party can take alone**.
> The strict condition: **each tier must return something at its own scale**; a tier returning nothing
> until the next is reached collapses the decomposition. *The personal tier is where this is most
> doubtful.*

> **Definition 13.3 / Claim 13.4 (Minimum viable adopting unit).** The smallest set of parties for
> which benefits exceed costs **without participation by anyone outside the set**.
> **Group tier = two parties who register each other's transitions. Open tier = one community with
> an implementation and a charter.**
> *A design whose minimum unit was a discipline requires an agreement nobody can broker. A design
> whose minimum unit is a seminar requires a decision two people can take on a Tuesday.*

**Claim 13.5.** The tier structure addresses **the coordination problem and not the cost problem**,
and the two are independent. Also: **cross-field matching is not decomposable** — it needs a
multi-field corpus, belongs to no tier, returns nothing to any adopting party, and **must play no part
in any adoption argument.**

## §14 Compatibility with the incumbent reward system

> **Requirement 14.1 (Compatibility).** An arrangement is adoptable only where a participant can
> obtain, **from a conforming record and without additional work**, an artefact of the kind the
> incumbent reward system accepts.

**Mechanism:** a `release` emits a document containing the released state, **the chain of transitions
leading to it**, the parties with their CRediT roles, the absorption links with attributions, and
**the objections standing at release**. Two economic properties: it requires **no additional work**
(assembled from records created in the course of the work); and it carries **an appendix no other
process can produce**.

**Claim 14.2.** Compatibility converts the decision from *a choice between two systems* into *an
addition to one*. **Three effects:** removes the sacrifice · supplies an immediate return **in the
currency that binds** (time recovered assembling a paper) · **defers organised opposition** (Olson:
opposition organises when a threat becomes visible).

**The objection that compatibility concedes too much** — participants keep publishing, publications
keep being counted, and the trajectory record becomes an unread appendix. **Answer is partial and
honest (Claim 14.3):** the objection is correct that the appendix may go unread indefinitely; what it
misses is the alternative — demanding abandonment of publication means accepting the dominance
argument's cost in full, so **the comparison is between an arrangement that may be ignored and one
that will not exist.** *That the first is preferable is a position, not a result.*

> **Claim 14.4 (the terminus).** Compatibility **postpones the conflict and does not remove it**, and
> **the conflict returns at exactly the point at which the arrangement begins to succeed.** A proposal
> claiming to threaten nobody while proposing to change what counts as evidence is **either naive or
> evasive** — this series states the terminus rather than claiming the conflict away.

## §15 What the empirical record actually shows

**Claim 15.1.** **No systematic study exists** of a defined population of research infrastructures
assessed against adoption outcomes. Available evidence = retrospective accounts of individual systems,
written mostly by associated parties, selected for attention because they succeeded or failed
conspicuously. **Infrastructures quietly built and quietly unused generate no literature at all — so
the population is unobservable exactly where the account makes its prediction.**

Pattern the discussed cases suggest (**Claim 15.2, conjecture**): infrastructures that **supplied a
tool researchers needed for their own work, with sharing as a byproduct of using the tool**, were
taken up (simulation/analysis platforms). Infrastructures **whose proposition was sharing** — the
contribution was the point and the return arrived through others — were built and **largely unused**
(workflow-sharing repositories).
**Claim 15.3 (conjecture).** Funding + institutional expectation are **sufficient to build** shared
research infrastructure and **not sufficient to produce voluntary use** — a mandate alters what a
participant must be *seen* to do, not the cost they bear.
**Claim 15.4.** No evidence that infrastructure increases productive unplanned encounters; the
strongest qualitative study reports **avoidance** (Irving et al. 2020).

> **Claim 15.5.** The empirical record **supports this paper's negative claims and none of its
> positive ones.** It gives reason to expect that arrangements without private benefit fail, and
> **gives no reason to expect that an arrangement with private benefit succeeds.**

## §16 Ten predictions (with refutations) — *the project's own evaluation plan*

**Adoption:** **P1** begins among parties insulated from output-rate selection, and among those whose
reporting burden is high relative to output · **P2** proceeds tier by tier — a substantial personal-tier
population **precedes** any group-tier population · **P3** arrangements adopted for a private benefit
persist; those adopted for a collective one do not.
**Behaviour:** **P4** where selection is present and absorbed content is *not* attributed, objection
is disproportionately destructive and strong material is disclosed late · **P5** where absorption is
attributed *and recognised*, contribution to others' lines rises · **P6** where selection is frequent,
plurality of criteria decays · **P7** **assessment capacity, not contribution volume, becomes the
binding constraint on any arrangement that succeeds.**
**Distribution:** **P8** overhead falls disproportionately on participants with least time, visible in
participation rates by career stage · **P9** maintenance concentrates on a small number of parties
whose number does not grow with adoption · **P10** attribution mechanisms are resisted by parties
benefiting from present invisibility, and **the resistance appears as objection to the mechanisms'
*accuracy* in place of objection to their *purpose*.**

**Refuting observations** are given for eight (e.g. adoption concentrated among parties under strong
output-rate selection without demonstrated private benefit refutes P1 *and the dominance argument
with it*; contribution volume rather than assessment capacity binding refutes P7 *and the location of
the commons dilemma*).

**Four measurement difficulties, all against the account's interest:** non-adoption is unobservable
(P1/P3 testable only among adopters); attribution recognition happens in non-public appointment and
funding decisions; destructive objection is surface-identical to rigorous objection; and **every
prediction concerns an arrangement that does not exist.**

> **Claim 16.1 — the prediction the account is most exposed on: P2.** If a substantial **personal-tier
> population appears and no group-tier population follows**, the tier decomposition will have
> delivered private utility **without the attested records on which every evidential claim in the
> series depends**, and the arrangement will have become **a note-taking convention**. This outcome is
> consistent with everything the account asserts and **would defeat the purpose the series was written
> for.**

## §17 Conclusion

**Five results:** location of the commons dilemma (four subtractable goods, not knowledge) · the
positional bound and the claims it forbids · open selection as a dissipating contest with the
absorption test as the distinguishing criterion · attributive absorption as the condition on staged
selection · private benefit as the condition on adoption.

**Four difficulties recorded as unaddressed** (no mechanism with an identified cost-bearer for whom
bearing it is rational): **assessment congestion** · **maintainer labour** · **the regressive
incidence of overhead** · **the recognition of attributed contribution**.

**Three arguments declined:** that the incumbent's defects justify any alternative · that adoption
follows from collective benefit · that the empirical record supports the proposal.

> The account is compatible with an outcome in which the arrangement is adopted by a small number of
> insulated parties, delivers private utility, never reaches the scale at which its evidential claims
> mean anything, and **is remembered as one more proposal of a kind that has been made since 1970.
> That outcome is stated here because the account predicts it, and because a paper that concealed it
> would exhibit the failure it spends its length diagnosing.**

## Implications for this repository

- **Never argue for adoption on collective grounds** in README, site copy, or applications
  (Claim 12.4). Argue: *this particular party, at this scale, gets back more than it gives up.*
- **Delete/never write:** "makes scholarship less competitive", "democratises recognition",
  "dissolves hierarchy" (Claim 6.2). Permitted: "changes what competition rewards".
- **Cross-field matching / similar-trajectory search must not appear in any adoption argument**
  (Claim 13.5) — it is a late corpus property that returns nothing to an adopting party.
  *This is why the retired plan's Stage 5 was struck.*
- **P2 is the project's own risk register entry:** ship the personal tier, but treat "personal tier
  adopted, group tier never" as the named failure mode, not a success.
- The **absorption test** should be a stated evaluation criterion for the project itself — and must
  **never be implemented as a counter or score** (Claim 8.4).
- The **release → citable document with lineage appendix** (Req. 14.1) is the single most
  adoption-critical feature. It belongs in M1, not a later stage.
- Roadmap items that add contribution volume without adding assessment capacity make P7 worse.
