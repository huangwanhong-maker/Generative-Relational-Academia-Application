# Notes — Paper III: *Grounds of Restriction and the Governance of Disclosure*

**Author:** Wanhong Huang · working draft, 2026 · CC BY-NC 4.0 · 38 pp, 15 sections
Source: [papers/GRA-paper-III-...pdf](../papers/GRA-paper-III-Grounds-of-Restriction.pdf)

**Role in the series:** the *disclosure governance* paper. An arrangement that records how
understanding changes **must be able to withhold**. The question is never *whether* but **on what
ground — and what follows from the ground**.

> Its single operative idea: **a ground determines an object and leaves a residue.** What a ground
> leaves *disclosable* is what distinguishes an arrangement that withholds **for a reason** from one
> that withholds **because it can**.

---

## Why ownership cannot supply the answer

Three reasons, inherited from the companion papers:
1. Legal instruments protect **finished products** — copyright/fixed expression, patent/completed
   invention *with disclosure*, trade secret/concealed information. A party's position **within an
   ongoing generation** is protected by none (Boyle 2008; Scotchmer 1991; Lemley 2012).
2. Rights to exclude, multiplied across fine-grained contributions, produce the **anticommons**
   (Heller & Eisenberg 1998).
3. **The design's own criterion runs the other way** — an arrangement in which value converges on a
   party *outside* the relation that produced it is exactly what the series diagnoses; exclusive
   rights over trajectory content would install that.

So **withholding is justified by a reason, never by a title.** And the reason must do work.

---

## §2 The instrument

- **Definition 2.1 — disclosure class.** A value attached to a record governing the set of parties to
  whom it may be disclosed. Classes are **ordered by inclusion**. Their identity and membership are
  settled by a community's **operating charter (L1)**, not by this account (Ostrom 1990,
  collective-choice arrangements).
- **Definition 2.2 — ground.** The declared reason a record is disclosed at less than the widest
  class available. **A ground is a property of a restriction, not of a record** — the same content may
  be restricted on different grounds in different arrangements, with different consequences.
- **Definition 2.3 — residue.** What remains disclosable at the widest class while a restriction on
  that ground is in force. **A ground with an empty residue licenses total silence.**

> **Requirement 2.4 (What a ground must state).** Four things: **(i) the condition** under which it
> obtains; **(ii) the object** it restricts; **(iii) the residue** it leaves disclosable; **(iv) the
> failure** produced by applying it where its condition does not obtain.
>
> *(iv) is what makes the instrument useful.* A ground stated without its failure mode **is a
> licence** — any party may assert the condition and no party can say what has gone wrong when the
> assertion is false.

> **Requirement 2.5 (Closed set).** The set of grounds is **closed**. A community **may not declare a
> new ground** in its charter; a restriction fitting none of the four is **inadmissible**.
> *Cost stated: a reasonable restriction fitting none of the four cannot be imposed — so the account
> may be wrong by omission. §14 invites exactly that observation.*

> **Requirement 2.6 (Disclosure of the residue) — the operative requirement of the paper.** Where a
> restriction is imposed on a ground, **the corresponding residue is disclosed at the widest class
> available.** A restriction withholding material its ground does not cover is **inadmissible,
> whatever the ground's condition.**
> *Without it every ground collapses into the same thing — silence with a justification attached.*

> **Requirement 2.7 (Assessment).** Three questions: **does the condition obtain? is the object
> restricted the object the ground identifies? has the residue been disclosed?** Failing any → the
> restriction is **misapplied**.
> Applied **by readers, not by the protocol** (the spec records the declared ground and enforces the
> class; it cannot assess whether the condition obtains). Applied **to a restriction, not to a
> party** — a party may restrict properly once and improperly the next time, and no inference runs
> from one to the other.

## §3–6 The four grounds

### The comparison table (Table 7.1)

| Ground | Condition | Object restricted | Residue | Named failure on misapplication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rivalry** | a resource admits limited simultaneous use (instrument, facility, compute, clinical population, budget line) | **access to the resource** | **the trajectory in full** — questions, methods, decisions, failures, interpretations, results | a restriction on **access** is converted into a restriction on **knowledge** |
| **Hazard** | propagation of the content creates risk of serious harm — the risk arises from the content **being known**, not from use of a resource | **the propagable content of the method**: the sequence by which a capability is obtained, the constraints discovered, the parameters that matter, the practical technique | existence of the work, questions pursued, decisions and their reasons, interpretations, **results that do not convey the method** | the **widest restriction on the strongest justification**, and it is **unfalsifiable from outside the restricted class** |
| **Exploratory vulnerability** | exposure at this stage would suppress or distort the work (premature criticism, appropriation, the participant's reluctance to record what may be read) | **the timing of exposure** | **everything, at the scheduled time** — the ground justifies delay and **no permanent withholding** | postponement converted into permanent withholding (schedule extended, or never set) |
| **Appropriability** | the work is funded by an arrangement requiring **excludability** | the content whose disclosure **would destroy the excludability the funding depends on** | existence, questions, decisions and their reasons, **and negative results** | **rent-seeking secrecy** — general secrecy obtained on an economic justification that does not apply |

### §3 Rivalry — the conversion that is "routine"

> **Claim 3.1 (formal).** Rivalry justifies selectivity **in access to the resource** and **no
> restriction on the record**. A record is non-rival, so the ground's condition does not obtain of it.

Labs with restricted instruments routinely restrict the surrounding record too — **and the practice
has reasons: appropriability, hazard, or vulnerability. Those are different grounds with different
residues, and they must be declared as themselves.**

> **Claim 3.3 — the paper's principal positive contribution on this ground.** Where an instrument is
> scarce, **the deciding process may be opened without opening the instrument.** Proposals, the
> reasoning behind them, the objections raised, the decision and its stated reason are all inside the
> residue, and disclosing them costs **nothing in the resource**. **What is opened is the direction of
> the work, not the capability to perform it.**

Two consequences: allocation decisions become inspectable; and **unsuccessful proposals become
available — which is the condition under which the absorption test (Paper II §8) can be satisfied at
all.** *An arrangement whose declined proposals are unrecorded cannot absorb from them.*
**Claim 3.4 — three limits.** It does not reduce dissipation (it converts dissipated effort into a
retained record, which is worth less than not dissipating it). It **exposes unsuccessful applicants**
(a declined proposal is a record of a judgement that did not persuade). And it is costless in the
*instrument* but **not costless** — it consumes **assessment capacity**, the binding constraint.

### §4 Hazard — where the design's central tension lives

> **Claim 4.1 (formal).** Where the hazard lies in **propagation of a method**, restricting access to
> an **instrument accomplishes nothing** — the instrument is frequently replaceable and often widely
> held. Rivalry and hazard restrict **different objects** and are **not substitutes**.
>
> This **disposes of the design the source discussion for this series proposed at length**:
> *restrict the instrument, open the reasoning.* That is correct under rivalry and **exactly wrong
> under hazard, since the reasoning is where the hazard lives.** (Bostrom 2011: the hazard attaches
> to **knowing**, not to **holding**.)

**Claim 4.2 — the residue is larger than practice usually allows.** That a line of work exists, what
question it pursues, what obstacles it met, what directions it abandoned and why, what its
participants take their results to mean: **none of these conveys a capability.** Withholding them on
the hazard ground is **misapplication**.
Practical arrangement for restricted work: **the trajectory is visible in its structure and its
reasoning; the propagable method is held at a narrow class.**

**Claim 4.3 — conditional access.** Following structured access (Shevlane 2022): a party **uses a
capability without receiving the artefact that confers it**, under conditions enforced by the
supplying party. Here: a party is **admitted to the restricted class under a charter's assurance
requirements, with the admission and its ground recorded.** This **converts a binary question
(disclose or not) into a question of whom to admit and under what commitments** — and the conversion
is what makes work under this ground possible at all.

**Four limits.** Hazard assessment **is not a competence a protocol has, and frequently not one the
participants have either** (hence institutional review). **Misapplication is undetectable from
outside** — a party outside the class cannot assess whether the restricted content is in fact
propagable, so the §2 standard is available only to parties inside the class or to a body a charter
empowers. The hazard is bounded by the **articulable fraction** — which bounds the benefit
identically. And:
> **Claim 4.4 — hazard has no natural terminus.** Rivalry ends when the instrument is free.
> Appropriability ends when the funding purpose is served. Vulnerability ends when the work matures.
> **Hazard does not end.** So **scheduled release is unavailable**, and the class must be maintained
> indefinitely by **parties who must continue to exist**. Where the community ceases, the material is
> held by nobody under any charter — **and the account offers no treatment of that case.**

### §5 Exploratory vulnerability — the timing ground

> **Claim 5.1 (Monotonicity).** Disclosure **may widen and cannot narrow** — a party who has read a
> record retains what they read. An arrangement offering the appearance of withdrawal **misdescribes
> to its own participants a state of affairs obtaining outside it.**
>
> Two consequences: **the default must be restrictive** (an error toward openness is uncorrectable,
> and defaults are where such errors occur); and **a schedule may be shortened and must not be
> extended** — *this asymmetry is the substance of the ground.*

**Claim 5.2 — the observer effect (conjecture).** Recording alters what is recorded. **The material
this series most wants preserved — half-formed ideas, real disagreement, the remark someone makes
before they are sure of it — is the material least likely to be produced under observation.** The
degradation falls precisely on the content the arrangement most values.
Mitigations, all partial and stated as such: narrowest default class, scheduled rather than immediate
release, participant-marked exclusions. *None removes the effect; an arrangement claiming otherwise
claims people behave identically observed and unobserved.*
**Structural mitigation = the unit of record.** What is preserved is *that an objection altered an
assumption* — **not that a particular party expressed it clumsily at a particular moment.**

**Claim 5.3 — the two populations.** A **well-positioned** party is exposed to criticism but not to
appropriation (standing protects the association of an idea with its author); delay serves them
**weakly**. A party **without position** is exposed to both, and **appropriation is the exposure that
matters**; delay serves them **strongly**, as does sealed registration. **So a charter setting short
delays uniformly imposes the exposure on the population least able to bear it.**

**Three limits.** Delay costs the work **the objections it would have received** — protected from
appropriation *and* from improvement, and the arrangement cannot supply the second without the first.
No objective terminus (a party may in good faith judge indefinitely that the work has not matured).
And it is **the easiest of the four to assert** — its condition concerns the state of a piece of work,
which only its participants can assess, so misapplication is detectable **only from a pattern of
schedules over time.**

### §6 Appropriability — legitimate, and most often declared as something else

**Claim 6.1.** The condition is **legitimate and not a regrettable concession**. Some work is not
performed unless somebody pays, and some funding requires excludability. Where that obtains,
**insisting on disclosure does not produce a disclosed trajectory; it produces no trajectory, because
the work does not occur.** (Arrow 1962 supplies the general form.)

**Claim 6.2.** The residues of hazard and appropriability **coincide in shape and differ in content**
— hazard asks *what does not convey a capability*; appropriability asks *what does not destroy
excludability*. **A negative result may be commercially harmless and hazardous; a question may be
commercially revealing and entirely safe.** Declaring one ground while applying the other's residue is
misapplication.
**Two residue items practice withholds routinely and shouldn't:** *the existence of the work* (rarely
what destroys excludability, and its disclosure is what lets others avoid duplicating effort) and
**negative results** (rarely what a competitor would have paid for — *and their disclosure is the
principal contribution the series claims for the reuse of abandoned material; this is the residue item
the series presses hardest*).

**Claim 6.3.** A party **holding a patent has exchanged disclosure for exclusion and may not invoke
appropriability over the disclosed matter.** Trade secret is the corresponding instrument. Copyright
is irrelevant (protects expression, not the excludable content). *The account adds nothing to what the
law permits and constrains what a party may declare within a conforming arrangement.*

> **Claim 6.4 — the strongest argument in the paper for the residue requirement.** Appropriability is
> **unverifiable from outside**, so misapplication is detectable **only through the residue**.
> **A party declaring appropriability and disclosing the residue has made a claim that costs them
> something; a party declaring it and withholding the residue has made a claim that costs nothing —
> and the second is the observable signature of misapplication.**
> *Where residues are disclosed, a false declaration is expensive; where they are not, it is free.*

Three limits: the terminus exists in principle and **rarely in practice** (nothing obliges release
once the funding purpose is served); the ground is **available only to parties with funding
arrangements**, so it is asymmetric **in favour of the well-resourced — a direction the series
generally resists and cannot resist here**; and assessment reduces to one question a reader can
answer — *was the residue disclosed?*

## §7 Irreducibility, composition, and how restrictions end

> **Claim 7.1 (Irreducibility).** No ground reduces to another. **Rivalry restricts a resource; the
> others restrict content. Hazard restricts what a *reader* could do; appropriability restricts what
> a *competitor* could sell — and the two select different content from the same trajectory.
> Vulnerability restricts a *time*; the others restrict a *set of parties*.**

Consequences: a restriction **must declare which ground it invokes** (object and residue follow from
the ground, not from the content); and **an arrangement using one ground for everything has either
misapplied it or is operating under a condition that does not obtain** — most commonly, declaring
*commercial sensitivity* over material that is actually restricted because it is dangerous, or early,
or because the instrument is booked.

> **Requirement 7.2 (Composition).** Where several grounds obtain, **each is declared separately, and
> the residue disclosed is the *intersection* of the residues of the grounds declared.** A party may
> not declare one ground and withhold on the strength of another.
> **Claim 7.3 — declaring more grounds is not free:** it narrows the residue **and raises the number
> of conditions the party asserts**, each of which can be assessed and found false.

*(Example of genuine composition: pharmaceutical development — funded by excludability, produces
material whose propagation carries risk, and involves early work exposure would distort.)*

> **Claim 7.4 — how each ground ends.** **Vulnerability** ends on a *recorded schedule* (shortenable).
> **Rivalry** ends when the resource is no longer contended — observable, unenforced.
> **Appropriability** ends when the funding purpose is served — *known to the declaring party alone*.
> **Hazard does not end.**
> Therefore **an arrangement resting mostly on the other three accumulates restricted material
> indefinitely**, and **a community that wishes its record to open over time must prefer the ground
> that ends.**

**Claim 7.5 — what the apparatus achieves (stated modestly):** a restriction becomes a **dated,
attributable assertion** where present practice makes it a default that asserts nothing; misapplication
becomes **describable** (each ground has a named failure, giving a reader a vocabulary); and the
**residue becomes the object of attention** — the informative question is not *was something withheld*
but **does what was disclosed match the ground declared**. **No enforcement is supplied.**

## §8 Simulation displaces the governance problem — it does not remove it

**Claim 8.1.** Where a simulated environment is faithful enough for exploration, the **rivalry ground
contracts to the validation stage**, and earlier stages open to any party with computation.
*(Genuine, with instances: community simulation → shared fabrication in hardware design; open
computational exploration → experimental validation.)*

> **Claim 8.2 — the displacement.** **A simulated environment is a selection environment.** What it
> admits, rewards, renders visible, and cannot represent determines which explorations succeed — so
> **the party who specifies the model and the benchmark occupies the position the party allocating the
> instrument previously occupied.**
> **The displacement is unfavourable in one respect:** allocation is **visible, episodic and
> contestable** (a decision is made, a party is refused, the refusal can be challenged); **benchmark
> specification is continuous, technical and rarely contested, and its effects appear as properties of
> the field rather than as decisions anyone took.**

**Claim 8.3.** Opening the benchmark is insufficient — **an open benchmark is a public attractor.**
Parties optimise for what it measures and the categories it recognises become the categories the field
maintains; inspectability of its assumptions does not prevent this. **Openness of a selection
environment makes its criteria visible and does not prevent them from governing.** *(Therefore a
simulated exploration stage is subject to Paper II's sparsity/plurality analysis — and a single widely
adopted benchmark applied continuously is both the worst case for reactivity and the arrangement a
field finds most convenient.)*

**Claim 8.4 — validation dependence.** A simulation's fidelity is established against the physical
apparatus, so **the open layer inherits a dependence on the closed one**; the parties who validate
hold a position over what the open layer may take as fidelity, **and the validation itself consumes
the scarce resource the proposal claimed to economise on.**

**Claim 8.5 — effect on hazard runs both ways.** Simulation **lowers** hazard where the hazard lies in
**execution** (exploration produces no dangerous artefact; the dangerous stage is confined and
supervised). It **raises** hazard where the hazard lies in **design** — an environment letting many
parties explore a design space cheaply lowers the cost of arriving at a dangerous design (*the
biological case is the one usually named*). **A proposal to displace exploration into simulation must
state which kind of hazard the domain carries.**

**Claim 8.6 — the qualification, and it is real.** Displacement **widens participation** in exploration
from parties with instrument access to parties with computation — a far larger and differently
distributed population. **This is the strongest reason to pursue the proposal, and the displacement
does not defeat it.**

## §9 The shared component — *the result that governs every restriction*

> **Definition 9.1 (The omitted component).** What a finished artefact does not contain: the
> approaches attempted and abandoned, the reasons for abandoning them, the constraints discovered,
> the parameters that mattered, **the practical technique by which a result was obtained.**

> **Proposition 9.2.** The omitted component carries **three properties at once**:
> **value in reuse** (what transfers is the record of what was tried and what obstruction was met) ·
> **evidential force** (a record of a completed result is cheap to fabricate; a record of the path is
> costly in proportion to the parties who attested it) · **hazard** (a propagable method is a sequence
> of steps and constraints, not a statement of a result).

> **Claim 9.3.** **No design achieves the epistemic benefit of an open trajectory while withholding
> the parts that carry risk, since the parts are the same parts.** A proposal to publish the
> trajectory minus its dangerous content is a proposal to publish the record minus what makes it worth
> publishing.
>
> **This is why the account is a typology of grounds rather than a filtering rule: the design cannot
> separate the valuable from the dangerous within the component, so it separates the *parties to whom
> the component is disclosed*.**

Per ground: under **hazard**, the residue lies *outside* the component, so a hazard restriction
**genuinely costs the commons something**. Under **appropriability**, same shape, different boundary —
**negative results usually fall outside the excludable content while remaining inside the omitted
component**, which is why the series presses them hardest. Under **vulnerability**, the component is
disclosed **later rather than never** — *which is why that ground costs the commons least*.

**Claim 9.4 — bounded together as well as carried together.** All three scale with the **articulable
fraction** (Collins 1974/2010; MacKenzie & Spinardi 1995). **A domain in which little of what matters
can be written produces records of little use, little evidential weight and little danger; a domain in
which much can be written produces records of much use, much weight and much danger. No design alters
the ratio.** *Practical use for a charter: where participants find little can be recorded, the
arrangement will deliver little and its restrictions may be correspondingly relaxed.*

> **Claim 9.5.** Conditional access preserves the component **for a class**; delay **postpones** it;
> and **the residues, being outside the component, are disclosable without touching the trade-off at
> all.** *This is why the residue requirement is the paper's operative rule: **it is the only part of
> the design that gains something without giving something up.** An account attending only to the
> component would find no room to move; the residue is where the room is.*

## §10 Downstream use — the structural gap

**Claim 10.1.** A record of how understanding changed, at scale, is an object of interest to parties
who did not produce it: **training material** (a corpus of recorded reasoning with typed transitions
and attached outcomes is precisely what a system learning to model reasoning would want) · research
material for the study of science · evaluation material for institutions · commercial material for
services. **The interest is proportional to the qualities the series claims — so the design's success
creates the interest.**

> **Claim 10.2.** **The four grounds govern *to whom* a record is disclosed and do not govern *what a
> party may do* with a record disclosed to them.** The account is therefore **incomplete with respect
> to downstream use, and the incompleteness is structural rather than an oversight.**

**Claim 10.3.** The labour of producing the record is borne by participants; the value of a corpus
assembled from many such records accrues to whoever assembles it. Where the assembler is
well-resourced and the producers are the population the series concerns, **value transfers from those
with least to those with most — which the design's own folding criterion marks as extractive, and
which the design supplies no mechanism to prevent.**

> **Requirement 10.4 (Terms of downstream use).** A charter **must state the terms** on which records
> disclosed under it may be reused by parties other than their producers, and **must state separately
> whether consent to disclosure constitutes consent to inclusion in a corpus assembled for purposes
> unrelated to the work.** **A record must carry the identifier of the charter under which it was
> disclosed**, so a party obtaining it can determine the terms.

Three matters a charter must decide (the account decides none of them): whether reuse *for study of
the work's own subject matter* differs from reuse *for training a system*; whether participants may
withhold consent to corpus inclusion while consenting to open disclosure — **and what a record looks
like where some have and some have not**; and whether a licence attaches, given that **licences
designed for finished works apply awkwardly to a graph of attributed transitions**.

**Claim 10.5 — three limits, the first defeating most of the attempt.** Terms attached to a disclosed
record are hard to enforce, and **the producing parties are least placed to bring the action**. Consent
to corpus inclusion is **not meaningfully separable in practice** — a term forbidding one use of openly
available material is a term whose breach is undetectable. And **restricting disclosure to prevent
assembly is not available**: it is a restriction on a ground the account does not admit, and it would
**impose the cost on the commons in order to withhold a benefit from a third party.** **Unaddressed.**

## §11 Who may restrict — the authority is itself a position

**Claim 11.1.** The authority to restrict **is a position** in the series' sense: constituted by acts
the arrangement requires, its holder determines what other participants may see, and others' capacity
to act depends on its exercise. *An arrangement designed to resist concentration cannot exempt the one
authority that determines what participants know.*

> **Requirement 11.2 (Recorded exercise).** Every **imposition, alteration and release** of a
> restriction is recorded as an **operation** carrying: the performing party, the **ground declared**,
> the **class imposed**, the **schedule** where one applies, and the **time**.
> *Cheap, and the whole of what the design can do here. It does not prevent improper restriction; it
> makes a party's pattern inspectable — the only basis on which misapplication can be detected at all.*

> **Requirement 11.3 (Separation of restricting from recording authority).** The party who **registers
> transitions** and the party who **determines disclosure classes** should be distinguishable in the
> record; a charter concentrating both must **state that it does so**. *A party holding both holds the
> record and its visibility together — and the record then evidences nothing about the party who
> produced it.*

> **Claim 11.4 — the series' general answer is weakest where the stakes are highest.** Portability
> bounds this authority **weakly**, because **a departing party can take the record they can see and
> cannot take what they were never shown.** The bound here is **charter and community, not exit.**

> **Requirement 11.5 (Charter statement of restricting authority).** A charter must state **which
> parties may restrict, on which grounds, at which classes, and whether any party may restrict without
> a schedule.**
> **A charter that leaves the authority unstated has left it with whoever operates the
> implementation.** *Authority not allocated is not absent; it is held by the operator — the party the
> series has least reason to trust.*

**Claim 11.6 — applied to the author.** An arrangement in which the proposing party maintained the
charter, operated the implementation, **and** held the restricting authority **would be an instance of
the concentration the series diagnoses**. Declaring that conflict is *a condition of the account's
coherence, not a courtesy.*

## §12 Detecting misapplication

> **Claim 12.1.** **Detectability of misapplication is inversely related to the width of the
> restriction the ground supports.** Ordering, most to least detectable:
> **Rivalry** (residue = trajectory in full, so any withholding is visible on its face) →
> **Vulnerability** (visible over time: extended schedules, or restrictions with no schedule) →
> **Appropriability** (detectable only through the residue) →
> **Hazard** (least detectable — the condition cannot be assessed without the content, and the content
> is what is withheld).
>
> **So hazard restrictions cannot be policed by readers.** A community relying on them must place the
> assessment with a **body it constitutes for the purpose — and must accept that the body then holds a
> position of the kind §11 treats.**

> **Claim 12.2 — the residue is the universally available observable.** For every ground, the question
> a reader can answer is **whether the residue was disclosed**. This **turns an unfalsifiable condition
> into a checkable one**: a party declaring hazard *and* disclosing existence, questions, decisions and
> interpretations **has behaved as a party with a genuine hazard would**; a party declaring hazard and
> disclosing nothing **has behaved as a party would who wanted silence and reached for the strongest
> available reason.**

**Claim 12.3.** **Individual restrictions are frequently ambiguous; patterns are not.** A party whose
restrictions are *always* on the same ground, *always* without a schedule, *always* at the narrowest
class, *always* without a residue, has a practice visible in the record even where no single
restriction can be faulted. **This is the design's principal contribution to disclosure governance —
and it is a contribution to visibility, not to control.**

**Claim 12.4 — three limits.** **Detection is not remedy** (the account supplies no procedure by which
a restriction is overturned). **Detection depends on someone looking** — and *the parties best placed
to look are frequently those with the least reason to.* And **a community that does not care will not
be made to care by a record**: every mechanism here produces information for parties who wish to use
it, and **none produces the wish.**

## §13 Jurisdictional variation

**Claim 13.1.** **Law is described and never encoded.** A specification encoding any jurisdiction's
rules would be **wrong somewhere immediately and obsolete everywhere within a few years.**
**Claim 13.2.** **Law reaches the design negatively, through structural incapacity — and that is the
only channel.** A data model incapable of expressing redaction forecloses lawful operation in every
regime with a right of erasure, and no charter repairs it (Reidenberg 1998; Lessig 2006).

**Four capacities that follow** (design properties, not legal conclusions):
1. **Separability** — personal content separable from the attested skeleton, so removal leaves the
   structure and its verification intact (Finck 2018; GDPR).
2. **Expressibility of redaction** — record *that* content was removed, when, by whom, on what ground,
   **without removing the record of the removal**.
3. **Consent as a recorded object** — a record concerning a person can reference a consent whose scope
   and date are recorded.
4. **Locality of storage** — record where material is held and restrict where it may be replicated.

**Claim 13.3.** These are **capacities, not rules.** An arrangement possessing them may still operate
unlawfully; one lacking them **cannot** operate lawfully where they are required. *The design's
obligation is to possess the capacities; lawfulness lies with implementers and their advisers.*

> **Requirement 13.4 (Charter statement of legal operation).** A charter must state: the
> **jurisdictions**, the **consent** required before a record concerning a person is created, the
> **retention** applying to each class, the treatment of **erasure requests**, and any restriction on
> **where material may be held**. Unstated → left to the operator.
> An arrangement spanning regimes holds several charters or one with regime-specific provisions, and
> **a record must identify which charter and version governed it when it was created.**

**Four limits (Claim 13.5).** Federation **distributes** the compliance burden and does not remove it —
*an implementation receiving records from another inherits obligations it did not create.* **Conflicting
obligations are not resolved** (retention vs erasure of the same material is a real conflict; the
implementation resolves it in a way it records, with no support from the spec). **Records already
propagated are not reached** — a redaction notice reaches implementations that receive it and no copy
held by a departed party. And **an arrangement cannot make its participants' own obligations
disappear.**

## §14 Predictions and falsification

**P1** hazard restrictions withhold more than the ground covers, more often than the other three
(← Claim 12.1) · **P2** where residues are required *and inspected*, appropriability declarations
**fall** (← 6.4) · **P3** arrangements **accumulate restricted material over time** (← 7.4) ·
**P4** communities will **declare vulnerability where appropriability obtains** — it is the easiest
ground to assert and carries no admission of commercial interest (← §5 limits) · **P5** displacement
into simulation is followed by **contestation over benchmarks**, concerning technical specification in
place of allocation (← 8.2) · **P6** third-party corpus assembly occurs wherever a substantial corpus
exists, **without compensation** (← 10.1, 10.3) · **P7** where restrictions are recorded and **nobody
inspects them**, restriction practice will **not differ** from arrangements that record nothing
(← 12.4).

Refuting observations given for all seven (e.g. *appropriability declarations unchanged in frequency
where residues are required and inspected refutes P2 — and with it the argument that the residue
requirement does any work*).

> **Claim 14.1 — what would show the set incomplete.** A restriction that **a competent and
> disinterested reader judges reasonable and that fits none of the four**. *The account invites the
> observation, since a typology whose exhaustiveness cannot be challenged is not a typology but a
> stipulation.*
>
> **Two candidates examined:**
> - *Protecting a third-party research subject* (medical/social research) — treated here as **hazard
>   with an identified party** rather than a general one. A reader who finds that reading forced has a
>   candidate.
> - *Honouring a confidentiality undertaking given to a supplying party* — fits appropriability **only
>   with strain**, since the excludability may protect the **supplier**, not the receiver. The account's
>   provisional answer (the receiving party declares appropriability on behalf of the supplier's
>   condition) is **strained, and this is the stronger candidate.**

**Three measurement difficulties:** restrictions in non-recording arrangements are unobservable;
**whether a residue was *disclosed* is observable, whether it was *complete* is not**; and
misapplication concerns a relation between a declaration and a condition only the declaring party can
assess, so it is testable **in aggregate patterns, not individual cases**.

---

## §15 Conclusion

**Six results:** restriction justified by **reason, not title** · **four irreducible grounds**, each
determining an object and a residue · **the residue requirement** as the barrier against a ground
operating as a licence *and* the only universally available observable · simulation **relocates**
governance from allocation to specification, unfavourably · **the shared component** of value,
evidence and hazard · **downstream use is governed by none of the four grounds.**

**Five unsettled:** closure of the set (confidentiality undertakings) · assessment of hazard · absence
of a terminus on three of four grounds · the weak bound on the restricting authority · enforcement of
downstream terms.

**Three claims declined:** that the four grounds are the *correct* set · that the apparatus *prevents*
improper restriction · **that openness is preferable to restriction** — each ground states a condition
under which restriction is legitimate, and appropriability is treated as *a condition of some work
existing at all* rather than as a concession.

> The contribution is **a discipline rather than a mechanism**. A reason, once declared, determines an
> object and leaves a residue, so a restriction becomes a dated attributable assertion whose adequacy a
> reader checks by asking **one question: was what the ground leaves disclosable in fact disclosed?**
>
> The account **stops at visibility, states that visibility without inspection changes nothing**, and
> leaves the inspecting to communities that may or may not perform it.
>
> **What the paper is most confident of is negative.** No design separates the valuable from the
> dangerous within a trajectory, since they are the same material. No ground supports a restriction
> wider than its object. **And no arrangement that withholds its residues is withholding for the reason
> it declares.**

## Implications for this repository

- Disclosure in the tool is **never a bare `--private` flag**: every restriction carries **a ground**,
  and grounds are a **closed set of four** (`rivalry | hazard | vulnerability | appropriability`) —
  which is exactly what `grrp disclose --class <c> --ground <g>` implements (Paper IV / M3).
- **Monotone disclosure (R11)** is a hard rule: **no `unpublish` operation exists**, and a schedule may
  be **shortened, never extended**. Enforce the direction, not just the field.
- **Vulnerability is the only ground with a recorded terminus** → `--release-at` applies to it
  naturally, is unavailable for hazard, and is unenforced for the other two. A UI that offers a
  schedule uniformly across grounds misrepresents the model.
- **Residue disclosure is the operative rule and should be surfaced by the tool**: when a ground is
  declared, prompt for / display *what the ground leaves disclosable* — this is the single highest-value
  affordance the account identifies, and it is the only thing that costs nothing.
- **Redaction must keep the record of the redaction** (Req. 11.2, §13 capacity 2) — matches the GRRP
  acceptance test "after `grrp redact`, the chain still verifies, the graph is unchanged, and the record
  of the redaction is present."
- **Records must carry the identifier of the governing charter and version** (Req. 10.4, 13.4) — the
  `charter.yaml` reference in `.grrp/` is doing this job; it must be **per-record-resolvable**, not just
  a repo-level file.
- **Downstream/corpus terms are a charter obligation and a known unaddressed gap.** If GRA ever
  publishes an aggregated corpus, Claim 10.3 says the design's own criterion marks uncompensated
  assembly as **extractive** — including if *we* do it.
- **Do not build "restrict the instrument, open the reasoning" as a general policy** — Claim 4.1 says it
  is exactly wrong under hazard.
